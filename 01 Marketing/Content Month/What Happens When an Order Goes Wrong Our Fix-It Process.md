1. Diagnose first: every issue gets sorted into shipping delay, production issue, buyer mistake, or unclear, the bucket decides the response
2. Acknowledge within hours, not days, the first reply is proof the order is being looked at, even before the answer
3. Pull the receipts: approved design file, QC records, carrier tracking, and the exact customization the buyer submitted
4. Resolve by cause: free priority reprint for our production errors, carrier escalation for delays, seller-led calls for buyer mistakes, replacement before refund
5. Close the loop and document it, so the same issue doesn't repeat for the next seller

## Slide copy

### Slide 1, Cover (hook)

**Headline (large):**
When a print order goes wrong, this is what we do.

**Subhead (small, optional):**
Our fix-it process, in full.

**Design notes:**
Biggest type on the slide. Short. No emoji, no exclamation point. Swipe-arrow in the corner. Keep it calm, the tone is "we've got a process," not "disaster."

---

### Slide 2, The honest truth

**Headline:**
Orders go wrong. In print-on-demand, that's not an exception, it's a category.

**Body:**
Misprints. Carrier delays. Buyer mix-ups. If you're selling, you'll see all of them eventually. The difference between a stressful business and a steady one isn't avoiding problems. It's having a process when they show up.

**Design notes:**
Two short paragraphs. Dense, not fluffy. Set up the rest of the carousel.

---

### Slide 3, Step 1: We diagnose before we react

**Headline:**
Step 1. Diagnose the issue.

**Body:**
Not every complaint is the same problem. We sort every issue into one of four buckets:
- Shipping delay, carrier is late, product is correct
- Production issue, what shipped doesn't match what was approved
- Buyer mistake, wrong size, address, or personalization submitted
- Unclear, not enough info yet

The bucket decides the response. We don't guess.

**Design notes:**
The four buckets should be visually stacked. Give each one a small visual weight, this is the most informative slide.

---

### Slide 4, Step 2: We acknowledge fast

**Headline:**
Step 2. Acknowledge within hours, not days.

**Body:**
The first message back to a seller isn't an answer. It's proof the order is being looked at. Something like:
"I'm pulling your order file now, I'll have details within [X] hours."
That one reply does more to calm a situation than a perfect solution sent two days later.

**Design notes:**
The pull-quote could be styled in a slightly different treatment (soft background block, italicized, whatever fits the brand kit). Keep it scannable.

---

### Slide 5, Step 3: We pull the receipts

**Headline:**
Step 3. Get the facts.

**Body:**
Before we resolve anything, we line up:
- The approved design file
- Production and QC records
- Carrier tracking and delivery scans
- The exact customization the buyer submitted

Clarity beats speed here. A confident answer built on receipts is worth more than a fast one built on assumptions.

**Design notes:**
Bulleted list reads cleaner than paragraph form here. Keep items short.

---

### Slide 6, Step 4: We make it right

**Headline:**
Step 4. Resolve, based on what actually went wrong.

**Body:**
- **Production issue on our end** → free reprint, produced on priority, typically out the door in 2, 5 business days.
- **Carrier delay** → we help the seller trace it and escalate with the carrier.
- **Buyer mistake** → we support the seller's own policy decision; we don't override it.
- **Unclear** → we keep digging until it isn't.

Replacement before refund, whenever it makes sense. A solved order is better for everyone than a closed one.

---

### Slide 7, Step 5: We close the loop

**Headline:**
Step 5. Close the loop, then learn from it.

**Body:**
Once the replacement ships or the refund clears, we don't just move on. We log what happened, why, and what we're changing so the same issue doesn't repeat for the next seller.
That's the part most fulfillment partners skip. It's also why the list of things that go wrong gets shorter over time.

**Design notes:**
Slightly more reflective tone here, but still concrete. This slide earns the CTA.
